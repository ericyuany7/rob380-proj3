Boogie, a dancing robot

A ROS-style forward kinematics runtime written in C++, driving an original 19-joint robot through a finite state machine choreography, drawn live in a three.js viewer.

The robot

Boogie is an original robot I described in URDF for this project. It is a tree of 30 links and 29 joints: 19 movable (8 continuous, 1 prismatic, 10 revolute) and 10 fixed joints for decorative parts such as the eyes, hubcaps and chest light. Every visual is a box, cylinder or sphere.

PartJoints
Wheeled base4 continuous wheels
Hiphip_lift, a prismatic joint that makes the body bounce
Torsowaist_twist (continuous) and torso_lean (side sway)
HeadPan and tilt, a wobbling antenna on a non-unit diagonal axis, and a spinning propeller
ArmsTwo arms, each with shoulder pitch, shoulder roll, elbow and a continuous wrist

The right arm is the left arm yawed by π, so the same joint values pose both arms as mirror images. That made the choreography much easier to write.

The choreography

The dance is a finite state machine with 22 states, saved as fsm/boogie_dance.json. Each state is a joint-space setpoint for all 19 joints. When the robot gets within the state's epsilon of that setpoint, the FSM follows the state's on_arrival transition to the next state. One loop takes about 28 seconds:

  1. Bounce: the prismatic hip drops and rises twice with the elbows bent, while the antenna wobbles and the head nods.
  2. Disco point: the right arm points up to the sky and back down, the torso sways and the head follows, with the left hand on the hip.
  3. Twist: arms out to the sides, the waist twists left and right and the wheels roll.
  4. The robot: elbows locked at 90°, the waist and head snap to one side, then the other.
  5. Wave: the right arm goes up and waves twice while the antenna wobbles.
  6. Helicopter: arms straight out, the waist turns a full 360°, the propeller and wrists spin and the hip rises.
  7. Jazz hands: the arms come up in front and the head tilts back.
  8. Bow: the head dips with one arm across the body, then back to home and the loop starts again.

I added an optional dwell field to each state (seconds to hold a pose before moving on) so the held poses read clearly on video. It extends the reference FSM format.

How it runs

The runtime is split into ROS-style nodes that talk over my Project 1 publish/subscribe middleware:

finite_state_machine  --/joint_trajectory-->  joint_state_publisher
joint_state_publisher --/joint_states------>  robot_state_publisher   (reads robot_description
robot_state_publisher --/tf---------------->  robot_world_state_publisher   from param_server)
robot_world_state_publisher --/xform_world-->  viewer

joint_state_publisher servos each joint toward the FSM's setpoint at 20 Hz, clamped to the joint's limits. robot_state_publisher turns each joint value into a parent-to-child transform T_origin · T_motion(q), and robot_world_state_publisher composes them down the tree into every link's pose in the world frame. All of the rotation math (roll-pitch-yaw, axis-angle, quaternions) is written by hand, without a kinematics library.

How I drew it

A browser cannot open the runtime's raw TCP/JSON socket, so a small Python bridge (tools/viewer.py, standard library only) connects to 127.0.0.1:9095 as an ordinary client. It subscribes to /xform_world, /joint_states and /fsm/status, and forwards them to the page as Server-Sent Events.

The page (tools/viewer.html) uses three.js. It parses the URDF to build one THREE.Group per link holding that link's visual geometry, with automatic matrix updates turned off. For every /xform_world message it copies each link's column-major 4×4 matrix straight in with Matrix4.fromArray, so the viewer does no kinematics of its own: what you see is exactly what my runtime computed. The page also has joint sliders, FSM controls (play/pause, jump to a state) and camera presets.
